About this Chronograph

Chronographs are long-run “living graphs”: one picture of a period you can scrub through, with an event card, an archive plate and a lane of leaders underneath. Siblings: The Dow, 1896–2026 and The Bank, 1694–1994. Series home: Chronographs.

The week spine follows the design team’s guide to the TimeGhost World War Two series presented by Indy Neidell (305 episodes; week 261 is missing from the playlists). Headlines and articles here are our own; each card links to the matching episode. The 24 weeks the guide marks “On the Big Board” are ringed in red.

Honest limits

  • Country fills and front lines are teaching approximations drawn on modern Natural Earth borders, stepped between 18 curated monthly snapshots: each week uses the latest snapshot dated (mid-month) on or before the week’s last day, so shading can lag events by weeks or months. They are not day-accurate. The World view colours the rest of the world by rough alignment only.
  • The guide’s introduction mentions 31 “Big Board” moments; only 24 rows carry the tag, and those 24 are the ones marked here. The pins and tags on Big Board weeks mark places named in the guide, not army positions.
  • Leader lanes show heads of government only (Hitler, Stalin and Chiang held power throughout). The notes are our own one-line judgements.
  • From 6 June 1944 the card shows the real HQ Twelfth Army Group situation map for that week (Library of Congress, public domain). We show it as a document; we have not re-drawn our own front from it, and we do not reconstruct any German or Allied “big board” beyond what those dated maps show. Weeks without a dated map say so.
  • The war-room clocks show nominal standard-time offsets for the week’s last day; wartime summer time and US “War Time” are not applied. The Battle of Britain plotting table (weeks 48–56) is illustrative, not a record of a real raid, and the convoy plot is drawn from the same rounded shipping series as the strip. Both insets appear in the War room view on wider screens.
  • The shipping line is a rounded monthly teaching series, not a primary statistic.

About the war room

The War room view borrows from the rooms where the war was actually followed: the Map Room in the Cabinet War Rooms under Whitehall, staffed round the clock from 1939 to 1945; the Fighter Command operations rooms at Bentley Priory and Uxbridge, where WAAF plotters pushed raid blocks across a table map; the Western Approaches convoy plot in Liverpool; and Roosevelt’s Map Room in the White House. Its pins, string and typed tags are a visual language, not a claim about what any of those boards showed on a given day.
